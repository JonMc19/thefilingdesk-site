    A 15% bond yield, a hot inflation report and the Nasdaq's worst week since June: AI's debt bill arrives

    CoreWeave's bonds started trading like distressed debt, inflation hit 3.7% and the Nasdaq fell 4.5% in five days. The boom was built on borrowed money. This was the week the price of that money went up.

    AI and interest rates By Jon Mc Gowan, The Filing Desk · October 18, 2026

    On Tuesday, a venture capitalist posted a chart on X. It showed that a $1.25 billion batch of bonds issued by CoreWeave, the AI cloud company backed by Nvidia, had briefly yielded more than 15%. A bond's yield rises when its price falls, and a yield that high is usually reserved for companies the market thinks might not pay it back. Elon Musk replied with one word: “Yikes.”

    By Friday's close, CoreWeave's stock had lost a quarter of its value for the week, CNN reported. The Nasdaq Composite, home of most big technology stocks, fell roughly 4.5% in five sessions, its worst week since June. Just ten days earlier it had closed at a record.

    The trigger was a single company, but the story is bigger than one company. The AI build-out, the data centers, chips and power lines that the industry says it needs, is being paid for with a great deal of borrowed money. On Wednesday a hotter-than-expected inflation report made that money look more expensive, and for longer. This was the week Wall Street stopped asking only whether AI demand is real and started asking who can afford to finance it.

    The subprime corner of the AI trade

    CoreWeave is what the industry calls a neocloud: a newer company that rents out computing power, mostly Nvidia chips, to AI developers. It has grown from three data centers to 51 in four years, according to SiliconANGLE, and it financed much of that with debt.

    How much? According to its second-quarter earnings release, CoreWeave had total debt of $35.6 billion and lease obligations of $16.3 billion, Fortune reported. In the first six months of the year it made $4.6 billion in revenue and lost $1.4 billion. Fortune put the question bluntly: how will a company that makes no money pay back $52 billion in combined debt and lease commitments?

    Gil Luria, head of technology research at D.A. Davidson, gave CNN his answer. “It’s unclear that (CoreWeave is) going to get a sufficiently good return to pay down the debt that they borrowed,” he said. “As people get concerned about the trajectory of the buildout, they are the subprime tranche of the AI trade, and that’s why they’re selling off first.”

    CoreWeave's chief executive, Mike Intrator, pushed back in a memo to employees, SiliconANGLE reported: “We are confident in our backlog which more than supports all of our debt obligations.” That backlog is large. CoreWeave has $103.7 billion of contracted future revenue, and 41% of it is scheduled to be paid by 2028, according to its own disclosure as quoted by Fortune. The catch is who signed those contracts. Much of the backlog is tied to Microsoft, Meta and OpenAI, and not everyone is reassured. Fortune noted that the company’s debt “dragged down the entire stock market this week.”

    On Wednesday, CoreWeave's stock fell 15.5%. On Thursday, Nvidia's chief executive, Jensen Huang, told Bloomberg he was optimistic about the company, and the shares rallied. It didn't last. On Friday they fell almost 12%. The stock is now down more than 65% from its peak shortly after its March 2025 listing, according to CNN.

    Then inflation came in hot

    What turned one company's problem into a market-wide selloff was Wednesday's consumer price report. Prices rose 3.7% over the year to September, with gasoline accounting for more than half of the monthly increase, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The war with Iran has pushed oil up all year, and on Friday Brent crude, the global benchmark, touched $110 a barrel for the first time since May, CNN reported.

    “Inflation is becoming increasingly difficult to dismiss as an energy-only story,” Chris Osmond, chief investment officer for Fifth Third Wealth Advisors, told Fortune. A day later, wholesale prices for September rose 1.3% in a single month, the largest monthly increase since March 2022.

    That matters because the Federal Reserve, which raised interest rates in September, now looks likely to raise them again. After the inflation report, traders priced in a 37% chance of a rate rise this month, up from 22% the day before, according to CME FedWatch data reported by Reuters. By Friday the odds for October had eased, but traders saw an 89% chance of a rise in December. The yield on the 10-year Treasury, the benchmark for mortgages and corporate loans, hit 5.33% after the report. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, asked about guidance, gave none. “It's a new chapter at the Fed, and we're not in that business anymore,” he said.

    For a company that owes tens of billions of dollars, every point on interest rates matters. Steve Sosnick, chief strategist at Interactive Brokers, summed up the problem to CNN: “There’s just so much money being thrown at this, and a lot of it has to be borrowed, and borrowed at increasingly high interest rates.”

    On Wednesday alone, Oracle, which has borrowed heavily to build data centers, fell 8.8%. Dell, which sells AI servers to neoclouds, fell 11.4% after a Barclays downgrade. A widely watched index of chip stocks fell 11.4% for the week, its worst since April 2025, as Chinese memory maker CXMT also announced progress on advanced memory chips. In South Korea, where two chipmakers dominate the market, the Kospi index fell 14.5%, its worst week since 2008.

    What the filings show

    The companies at the top of the AI build-out can pay for it from their own cash. The ones a step down often can't. Oracle is the clearest example in our data. In its fiscal year that ended in May, Oracle's capital spending reached $55.7 billion, according to its annual report, while its operations generated $32.0 billion in cash. The gap, about $23.7 billion, had to come from somewhere, and much of it came from borrowing. Microsoft, by contrast, spent $115.9 billion in its latest fiscal year but generated $182.9 billion from its operations.

    Companies are also talking about AI more. Our count of 8-K filings, the reports companies must file when something important happens, found 92 filings mentioning artificial intelligence this week. As a share of all 8-Ks, that was 27% above the usual week and the highest rate in the nine weeks we track.

    Some insiders at AI hardware companies sold shares just before the selloff. Jayshree Ullal, chief executive of Arista Networks, which makes the network switches that link AI chips together, sold $29.5 million of stock on October 8 and 9, according to her Form 4, under a 10b5-1 trading plan adopted last November. A Silver Lake fund, a longtime Dell owner, sold $29.4 million of Dell stock on October 9, according to its filing. John Colgrove, a director of data-storage maker Everpure, sold $45.4 million on October 12 through family trusts, under a plan adopted in January, his Form 4 shows. Together that is $104.2 million. Pre-arranged plans like these are set up months in advance, precisely so that executives can't time the market, and insiders sell for many reasons. The sales are a record of who was cashing out, not a verdict on the companies.

    Our read: not a bust, a repricing

    This is analysis, not a forecast of any share price. We don't think this week showed that AI demand is fading. CoreWeave's backlog is real, and the biggest buyers of computing power are still spending. What changed is the price of money. A boom that looked cheap to finance with rates near zero looks very different with the 10-year yield touching 5.33%.

    The likely result is a split. Companies that pay for data centers out of their own profits, like Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta, can keep building. Companies that depend on borrowing, the neoclouds, crypto miners turned AI hosts, and to a degree Oracle, face higher costs just as investors start asking harder questions. The International Monetary Fund flagged the same risk on Tuesday: an AI-related correction in stock markets is one of the dangers to growth, its deputy director Athanasios Vamvakidis said, along with rising bond yields.

    For everyone else, the link runs through prices. If inflation keeps rising, the Fed raises rates, mortgages and car loans get dearer, and the AI build-out slows at the margin, which could ease pressure on electricity bills in the places where data centers cluster. If the CPI report in November cools and the Fed holds in December, this week will look like a scare. Watch CoreWeave's bonds: if their yields fall back toward single digits, the market has decided the debt is safe. If they keep climbing, the questions will spread to bigger names.

    Melissa Brown of SimCorp put the longer worry in a note on Friday. When a theme drives stock weights above about 40% of the market, she wrote, “it does seem to eventually be associated with a market peak.” Then she added: “The U.S. market is there now.” The AI boom isn't over. But for the first time in a while, it has a bill to pay.
