    A second affair report puts Jensen Huang's job in doubt as Nvidia's board weighs his future

    Days after Huang admitted an affair, The New York Times reported another, with an Nvidia executive who reports to him. The board met late Wednesday to decide whether he stays, and by Thursday morning it had announced nothing.

    Nvidia By Jon Mc Gowan, The Filing Desk · November 12, 2026

    On Sunday, Nvidia's board said it had confidence in Jensen Huang. By Wednesday night, it was meeting to decide whether to fire him.

    The turn came in a few hours. After the market closed on Wednesday, The Wall Street Journal reported that the board was preparing to force Huang out, minutes after The New York Times reported that he had a romantic relationship with an Nvidia executive, CNBC reported. That makes two: on Sunday, Huang told his staff he had been unfaithful to his wife, after reports that U.S. intelligence agencies believe the woman involved, Alice Huang (no relation), spied for China. Our story on Monday covered how that began and what Nvidia's filings say about the risk.

    The board met late on Wednesday, but "no decision had been announced by the end of the night," according to a source who spoke to Reuters, as reported by TNW. Nvidia had filed nothing new with the SEC by early Thursday. Here is what changed this week, what Nvidia's own filings say about who decides, and what to watch next.

    From an inquiry to a vote on the CEO

    The board's tone shifted over three days. On Tuesday, its independent members said they had hired Joon H. Kim, a former acting U.S. attorney in Manhattan, and his law firm, Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz, "to conduct a thorough and independent investigation." NBC News read the choice as a signal that the board "does not intend its probe to serve as a rubber stamp for Huang’s actions," noting that many directors "have worked alongside Huang for decades and become wealthier with him."

    Then came Wednesday's report. The Times said that Huang "also allegedly had an extramarital relationship with an Nvidia executive who allegedly reports to him," and that the two were allegedly filmed "being affectionate at a hotel during an Nvidia conference in March," ABC News reported. The Times said it had not seen the video, and ABC said it could not verify the reporting. Asked about it by phone, Huang told the Times, "I don’t think you should be talking to me directly," before hanging up, according to TNW. Nvidia did not respond to requests for comment. We aren't naming the executive: she hasn't commented, and the reported relationship matters here for what it means for Nvidia's governance.

    Two sources told CBS News the board is "preparing to oust CEO Jensen Huang soon." Some directors favor naming Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress as interim chief executive, and the board has discussed adding a chief operating officer alongside her, Semafor reported, citing people familiar with the matter. Nvidia has hired an outside search firm to find its next CEO, Bloomberg reported, according to Semafor. A senior administration official told Semafor that the board had briefed the White House on its meeting, and two sources told CNN that some Trump administration insiders expected Huang to be ousted.

    Who decides, according to Nvidia's filings

    Huang is not the board's chair, and neither is anyone else. Nvidia's latest proxy statement says: "Our Board ensures that each member has an equal voice in the affairs and management of NVIDIA by having an independent Lead Director, rather than a chairperson." That Lead Director is Stephen C. Neal, on the board since 2019, whose listed experience includes being a trial attorney. The same filing gives the chief executive "primary responsibility for the operational leadership and strategic direction of the Company."

    The rules make removing him simple on paper. Nvidia's bylaws let a majority of its 11 directors dismiss the CEO with or without cause, and all of them except Huang are considered independent, TNW reported. The practice is harder. Huang owns around 3.6% of the company and built it, worth roughly $4 trillion, "without putting a succession plan in place," according to TNW. Nvidia's 10-K names that as a risk: "Failure to ensure effective succession planning, transfer of knowledge, and smooth transitions involving executives and key employees could hinder our strategic planning, execution, and long-term success."

    In the fiscal year to January 2026, Nvidia's revenue was $215.9 billion and its net income $120.1 billion, according to its filings.

    The pressure from Washington and the courts

    Outside the boardroom, the questions have multiplied. President Trump said on Monday that "the FBI is looking into it in terms of something else, and I think that he'll be in good shape," CBS News reported. Trump said Huang "had absolutely no idea that the person in question was a spy," and added: "I really believe him," NBC News reported. The Times reported that Secretary of State Marco Rubio had warned Trump in May, aboard Air Force One on the way to China; asked when he first heard, Trump said: "They could have been mentioned," and "Certainly, it didn’t make much of an impact on me," NBC News reported.

    Two law enforcement sources told CBS News the Justice Department is also investigating Megaspeed, the Singapore company Alice Huang helped run, over possible breaches of U.S. export rules on AI chips. Megaspeed has denied wrongdoing and calls itself a "compliant and legal company," CNN reported. Inside the administration, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent challenged Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick last week over his department's reluctance to label Megaspeed a possible national security threat, two people told CNN.

    Congress wants Huang in person. Republican committee chairs have asked him to a closed-door briefing on November 18, and Rep. Brian Mast said he was prepared to subpoena him if he didn't respond, ABC News reported. "I think he has this belief that it’s the United States of Nvidia and not the United States of America," Mast said, according to Fox News. Democratic Senators Chris Coons and Elizabeth Warren asked Cabinet members to preserve documents about Nvidia's chip exports, writing that "This incident raises significant concerns about the federal government’s ability to effectively enforce our export controls and protect our national security," Semafor reported. Nvidia also faces a securities lawsuit accusing it of not telling shareholders about Huang's relationship with the alleged spy, CBS News reported.

    Alice Huang has returned to China, Bloomberg reported on Wednesday night, according to CBS. Asked about it on Thursday, a Chinese foreign ministry spokesperson, Mao Ning, said: "I do not have information on what you mentioned." Mao also said "The US should stop fabricating so-called espionage narratives and using them as an excuse to impose a technological blockade on China," CNN reported. Nvidia's spokesperson John Rizzo told CBS News: "We trust that if we were the target of a genuine threat, the U.S. government would have notified us."

    How the market took it

    Nvidia's shares fell sharply in after-hours trading on Wednesday after the Journal's report, CNBC and CBS News reported. They were already down 7% this week before that drop and on course for a sixth straight weekly fall, CNBC reported, and had dropped 31% from their October 6 record by Tuesday, according to NBC News.

    Analysts disagree on how much the outcome matters. Bernstein analysts wrote: "With the new allegations, it feels impossible to us for the board to keep him in the role," Yahoo Finance reported. Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives, who on Monday had called talk of replacing Huang "comedy," wrote that "the writing is on the wall that Jensen's days at Nvidia could be numbered." Simon Leopold of Raymond James took the other side of the leadership question: "we argue that Nvidia is more than just its CEO." And Armina Rosenberg of Minotaur Capital, an Nvidia shareholder, told Reuters: "The market is starting to price Nvidia without its founder as the likely outcome, not a tail risk."

    Our read

    This is our analysis. On Monday we wrote that a move to name an interim chief would mean this was more than a bad week. The board is now discussing one, according to Semafor. The second report changes the question the board has to answer. The first was about national security, which Nvidia said it had no reason to think was harmed and which a former prosecutor will now examine. The second is about a reported relationship with someone who works for him, which boards judge for themselves, and usually quickly.

    We expect three things. First, the decision will come before November 17, when Nvidia reports its third quarter, because few boards want a quarterly call where the main question is who runs the company. Second, whatever is decided, the first official word will most likely come in a filing with the SEC; until then, everything is reporting from people who weren't named. Third, the quarterly report that follows will tell us how Nvidia now describes these risks: watch for the investigation, the lawsuit and the export questions in its risk factors, next to the line on succession planning it already has. We would be wrong if Huang stays as chief executive with no change to his role, or if the inquiry clears him before the board acts.

    On Sunday, Nvidia's biggest strength and its biggest risk were the same man. By Thursday morning, its board was deciding whether it can keep one without the other.
