    Jensen Huang admits an affair as Nvidia's board opens a spy inquiry. What its filings say is at stake

    Nvidia's chief told staff he was unfaithful to his wife, and the board is investigating reports that he was the target of Chinese intelligence. Nvidia's own 10-K says it is highly dependent on its longstanding executive team.

    Nvidia By Jon Mc Gowan, The Filing Desk · November 9, 2026

    On Sunday afternoon, the chief executive of one of the world's two most valuable companies sent his staff an email that began: "I was unfaithful to my wife. My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did. I am deeply sorry."

    Jensen Huang, who co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and has run it ever since, did not name anyone. He didn't need to. The same afternoon, Nvidia's board said it was opening an independent investigation "in light of recent reporting that founder and CEO Jensen Huang has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort."

    That reporting, by The New York Times and the Financial Times, concerns Alice Huang (no relation), an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company that bought Nvidia's chips. According to the newspapers, as summarized by NBC News and CNBC, U.S. intelligence agencies concluded that she had been recruited by China's top intelligence agency. China denies it. None of it has been tested in public. But it lands on the man most identified with the AI boom, at the company whose chips it runs on, worth more than $4 trillion, CNN reported, and at the center of the fight between Washington and Beijing over who gets those chips. Here is what is known, what Nvidia's own filings say about this kind of risk, and what to watch next.

    What Nvidia said on Sunday

    Huang's email, which Nvidia posted on its news site, moves from his family to his company. To employees, the board, partners and shareholders he wrote: "I have fallen short of that responsibility. I am fully committed to NVIDIA and will work to earn back your confidence." On the investigation: "I support the investigation and will cooperate fully." He closed with a request: "My family bears no responsibility for my choices. I do. Please give them privacy."

    The board's statement was short. A committee of independent directors, helped by outside lawyers, will run the inquiry. It also said what it believes so far: "While the Board has no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised, the Board's inquiry will examine all available information." And it backed its chief: "The Board has confidence in Jensen and his leadership team and will provide further updates as appropriate."

    A company spokesperson went further, telling CNN: "Today's news will not slow NVIDIA. Jensen is committed to continuing to lead NVIDIA as CEO without interruption."

    Who is Alice Huang, and what is Megaspeed?

    Megaspeed rents out computing power. CNBC describes it as selling "access to computing resources hosted in data centers in Southeast Asia," mostly to companies in the Asia Pacific that need it for AI work. That matters because U.S. export rules stop Nvidia's most advanced chips from being sold into China, and Megaspeed has been accused of helping restricted Chinese companies get them, CNBC has reported. According to Bloomberg, as cited by CNBC, Alice Huang, who also goes by Huang Le, helped set Megaspeed up in 2023, shortly after the first U.S. bans on chip exports to China.

    The intelligence claims come from people who spoke to reporters without being named. The Times reported on Friday, according to CNBC, that a memo circulated in the Trump administration last spring identified a Chinese businesswoman close to Jensen Huang as "passing information about U.S. AI chips to Beijing's top spy agency and seeking policy influence." Late on Sunday, a person familiar with the matter told NBC News that U.S. agencies had determined she was a spy and had a relationship with Jensen Huang. What, if anything, she passed on has not been reported.

    Everyone else involved has denied wrongdoing. Megaspeed has said inspections by the U.S. Commerce Department, Malaysia's trade ministry and Nvidia found "no evidence of any violations concerning the illicit transfer of chips or any illegal activities." Nvidia had called diversion concerns about the company "debunked last year," CNN reported, and said Huang's meetings were "not unusual or unexpected." Huang himself, before Sunday, described the relationship as business: "Our relationship, our interactions, have been related to Megaspeed, and it was all completely normal and not unusual." On Monday, China's foreign ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun called the allegation "purely groundless speculation and an attempt to smear and malign China," according to CNN.

    Washington wants answers

    President Trump stood by Huang. Asked whether he wished he had acted on the intelligence, he told reporters: "No I don't think so. I think he's an honorable guy. I think he probably has an excuse for it." He added: "He's done an amazing job for our country." Secretary of State Marco Rubio had warned the White House about the relationship before Trump's trip to China in May, which Huang joined, according to the Times as reported by TODAY. Rubio told reporters on Sunday he couldn't comment.

    Congress is less patient. Three Republican House committee chairmen had already asked Huang last week to attend a closed-door briefing on November 18. "Recent reports raise significant national security concerns regarding the PRC's access to sensitive US technology and information," they wrote. Democrats, who won both chambers in last week's midterm elections, are about to take over the gavels. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries called it "a significant issue of concern," and Senator Chris Coons wrote that "Congress will investigate and get answers," CNBC reported.

    What Nvidia's own filings say

    Nvidia's annual report lists the risks the company itself considers serious, and two of them read differently this week. The first is about people. In its 10-K for the year to January 2026, under the heading on attracting and keeping executives, Nvidia wrote: "Additionally, we are highly dependent on the services of our longstanding executive team. Failure to ensure effective succession planning, transfer of knowledge, and smooth transitions involving executives and key employees could hinder our strategic planning, execution, and long-term success." The same filing says Huang has been president, chief executive and a director "since our inception."

    Analysts make the same point more bluntly. "Nvidia is not an ordinary company and Jensen is not an ordinary CEO; he IS Nvidia," Bernstein's Stacy Rasgon wrote on Sunday, adding that it isn't clear who would succeed him. Huang is not the board's chairman: he is one of its eleven members, and there is no designated chair, CNBC noted. That leaves the decision about him with independent directors.

    Under Huang, the company's yearly revenue grew from $6.9 billion in the fiscal year to January 2017 to $215.9 billion in the year to January 2026, according to its filings.

    The second risk is about China. In its latest 10-Q, Nvidia warns that "reports of diversion of controlled products, even when unsubstantiated and untrue, or any compliance failure at a customer or partner, may negatively impact our business, relationships with partners and customers, and our reputation." The same report shows how little China now adds directly: sales of the H200 chips the U.S. licensed for China-based customers "account for less than 1% of Data Center revenue in our most recent quarter."

    How the market took it

    Nvidia's shares were falling in Monday trading while the broader market rose, CNBC and NBC News reported. They had already plunged 12% in the two days after the election, CNBC reported, on fears that the new lawmakers would slow the AI buildout.

    Views differ on how much this matters. Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives & Co. called it "a black eye moment for Jensen" that will bring more scrutiny of chip sales to China, but said talk of replacing Huang is "comedy." Rasgon wrote that "demand for the company's products seems unlikely to be affected," but that it "seems plausible to postulate increased risks (regulatory etc.) as AI buildouts become an increasingly political issue." CNN contributor Jacob Ward said boards have historically tended to oust chief executives caught in affairs within days, and one investor close to Huang told CNBC he could foresee a leave of absence, depending on what the inquiry finds.

    Our read

    This is our analysis. The affair is a private matter, and on its own it would probably be a bad week, not a turning point. What makes it a corporate event is the question the board has taken on: whether anything that matters to Nvidia or to the United States left the building. Nvidia has said it has no reason to think so. It has not yet said it knows.

    We expect three things. First, the pressure lands on Nvidia's China ambitions more than on its sales. Its licensed sales of H200 chips to China are already tiny, by its own account, and a chief executive under investigation for a suspected Chinese intelligence link will find it much harder to argue for looser export rules in a Congress run by his critics. Second, the board will be pushed to show it has a plan if Huang has to step aside, something its own 10-K names as a risk. Third, the next two weeks will set the tone: Nvidia reports its third quarter on November 17, and Huang is due before House committee leaders the day after. We would be wrong if the inquiry ends quickly with a clear finding and Congress moves on; we would be wrong the other way if the board names an interim chief or any official confirms that information was passed.

    For three decades, Nvidia's biggest strength has been one man. This week, its own filings and its own board are being tested on what happens when that is also its biggest risk.
