    Nebius's revenue jumped 571% to $980.8 million, but it now sees 2026 at the low end and will spend more

    The AI cloud company beat forecasts, helped by one-off revenue. Delays push some growth into 2027, and its spending plans rose far above what analysts expected. The shares fell before the open.

    Nebius By Jon Mc Gowan, The Filing Desk · November 12, 2026

    Nebius sold far more computing power than Wall Street expected in its third quarter, and then told investors that part of its growth will come later than promised and cost more to build. Revenue jumped 571% to $980.8 million, the Amsterdam-based AI cloud company reported before the market opened on Thursday, beating the roughly $907.2 million analysts expected, Rolling Out reported.

    But Nebius now expects 2026 revenue at the low end of its range, and raised what it plans to spend, CNBC reported. Premarket trading swung on the news: shortly after the release, the shares were marginally higher in volatile trading, Reuters reported, but within the hour CNBC and Investing.com reported them sharply lower, and other AI cloud companies' shares fell with them.

    The results come a day after Nebius's shares rose 15.44%, CNBC reported, on strong results from its rival CoreWeave, leaving them up 85% this year as of Wednesday's close, according to Asianet Newsable. Our preview set out what to watch. Here is what Nebius reported, what changed in its outlook, and what it means.

    A big quarter, with a one-off inside it

    About $325 million of the quarter's revenue, a third of it, was non-recurring, The Energy Mag reported: money from partnerships that deploy Nebius's technology on data centers other companies pay for. Without it, revenue would have been well short of what analysts expected. The core AI cloud business brought in $973.8 million with an adjusted EBITDA margin of 53.2%.

    Adjusted EBITDA, earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization and some other items, the company's preferred measure, reached $465.8 million, against a loss a year earlier. By standard accounting, Nebius still lost money: a net loss of $37.5 million, narrower than $119.6 million a year earlier. Operating activities used $273 million in cash in the quarter, while capital spending totaled $7.3 billion.

    What moved in the outlook

    In August, Nebius had reaffirmed 2026 revenue of $3 billion to $3.4 billion, The Energy Mag reported. Now it expects the low end; analysts surveyed by FactSet had expected $3.35 billion, according to CNBC. The reason is timing: some capacity that partners were to bring online this year is now scheduled for the first half of 2027, while the customer contracts remain in place, the company said, as reported by The Energy Mag. That includes its New Jersey site, where it is switching the power source to Bloom Energy fuel cells. The year-end revenue run-rate it had promised for December, $7 billion to $9 billion, is now expected to pass $7 billion in February.

    Spending goes the other way. Nebius now expects 2026 capital expenditures of $27 billion, above its earlier $20 billion to $25 billion, and $40 billion to $45 billion in 2027, far above the $28.77 billion analysts expected, CNBC reported. Its first revenue outlook for 2027 is $12 billion to $13 billion, close to the $12.09 billion FactSet expected. It aims to reach pre-tax break-even in the fourth quarter of 2027.

    Customers are paying for much of the build-out up front. They have committed more than $12 billion in contract value since the start of the third quarter, and Nebius expects prepayments and borrowing against contracts with investment-grade customers to cover 75% to 85% of next year's spending, The Energy Mag reported. From 2027 it will stop guiding on run-rate revenue and guide on revenue, pre-tax earnings and capacity instead.

    What it means

    This is our analysis, not a forecast of the shares.

    The quarter answers one worry and sharpens another. Demand does not look like the problem: customers are signing bigger deals and paying much of the bill in advance. The problem is timing and money. A business that rents out computing power can only book revenue once the power and the buildings are ready, and this quarter showed how much of that depends on partners Nebius does not control. At the same time, the spending plan for next year grew far faster than analysts expected, while revenue guidance only matched them.

    Three things will tell whether the setback is temporary. First, whether the delayed capacity, New Jersey included, arrives in the first half of 2027 as promised. Earlier this week, the shares had already fallen on reports that Microsoft may scale back its partnerships with companies like Nebius, Investing.com reported; a site running late adds to that question. Second, whether the partnership revenue that made up a third of this quarter recurs, as Nebius expects. If it does, it is a second engine that needs less of Nebius's own capital. If it doesn't, this quarter's beat will look like a one-off. Third, whether prepayments and borrowing keep pace with a spending plan of up to $45 billion.

    Nebius runs on Nvidia's chips, and Nvidia held about $329 million of its shares at the end of June, CNBC reported, in a week when Nvidia's board is deciding the future of its chief executive. Our story has the latest on that.
