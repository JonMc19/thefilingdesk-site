    Meta's AI bill: cash flowing out, a new stock with no vote, and the Nasdaq's worst day since June

    Meta spent more on data centers than its business brought in, then created a class of shares that can raise money without costing Mark Zuckerberg control. Tesla went the other way. Investors didn't like either.

    AI spending By Jon Mc Gowan, The Filing Desk · October 25, 2026

    On the evening of September 9, Mark Zuckerberg sat down to dinner with Meta's board and told the directors he planned to put his Palo Alto estate up for sale four days later, Fortune reported, drawing on Meta's own filing. The board members negotiating with him had left the room for that part. Within days they had a deal.

    The world saw that deal on Wednesday, October 21, stapled to Meta's third-quarter results: a new class of Meta stock with no votes attached, and a promise from Zuckerberg to stay in California for eight years. The same release showed something else. For the quarter, Meta spent more on data centers and servers than its business brought in.

    On Thursday, investors delivered their verdict. Meta's shares fell nearly 7 percent, and the Nasdaq Composite fell 2.6 percent, its steepest fall since the index tumbled 4.2 percent on June 5, Al Jazeera reported. This is the story of why one company's paperwork moved the whole market, and what it says about who will pay for the AI build-out.

    The quarter Meta's cash ran out the door

    The business itself is booming. Meta's revenue rose 26% from a year earlier to $64.6 billion, according to its quarterly report. Profit was dented by a $9.89 billion charge for its settlement with state attorneys general over teen mental health. “Our investments are paying off, the core business is strong, and we're seeing velocity across the board,” Zuckerberg said in the earnings release.

    The investments are the problem. Meta's capital expenditures, the money it spends on buildings, chips and servers, came to $34.91 billion in three months. Its operations generated $32.79 billion of cash. The difference is free cash flow, the cash left after paying for that equipment, and it came out at negative $2.12 billion. A year earlier it was a positive $10.63 billion, according to Zacks Investment Research, which called it “the most concerning fact” of the report and said it was Meta's first quarterly outflow of free cash flow in its history.

    Meta narrowed its forecast for this year's capital spending to $135 billion to $145 billion, from $130 billion to $145 billion, SiliconANGLE reported. For scale: its annual filings show it spent $37.3 billion in 2024 and $69.7 billion in 2025. And it has signed up for much more. As of September 30, the 10-Q says, Meta had $454.52 billion of non-cancelable contractual commitments, most of them for cloud capacity and other technical infrastructure.

    A share with no vote

    Something has to fill that gap, and Meta has stopped one of its old habits to make room. It bought back $26.2 billion of its own stock in 2025, but its 10-Q says: “We did not repurchase any shares of Class A common stock during the nine months ended September 30, 2026.” It has also borrowed, as last week's story on the AI industry's debt explained.

    Now comes stock. Every holder of Meta's Class A and Class B shares will receive one new Class C share for each share they own, with the new class taking effect on January 19, 2027. It works like a two-for-one stock split. The difference is that Class C shares carry no vote. Meta can later sell more of them for cash, pay staff with them or use them to buy companies, without diluting Zuckerberg's grip. He holds about 60.6% of the votes on a stake of about 13.4% of the company, according to Fortune.

    Meta's information statement, filed with the SEC the same day, explains the reasoning in unusually blunt terms. “The scale of investment required to compete, sustain our growth, expand our infrastructure, and pursue new strategic initiatives amidst the dramatic transformation taking place in the tech industry and global economy related to artificial intelligence is unprecedented,” it says. It records that management's long-term forecasts, shown to the board in May, “showed the Company’s need to raise hundreds of billions of dollars over a period of several years beginning as early as fall 2026.” The board's adviser, Moelis, calculated that Meta could raise about $700 billion in equity over several years before Zuckerberg's voting power fell below 50%.

    Chief financial officer Susan Li played that down. On a call with analysts she called the “hundreds of billions” figure “a theoretical maximum,” Decrypt reported, and said Meta had no specific financing plans yet. “We thought it was prudent to establish the appropriate capital structure framework to ensure that we have access to a diverse pool of funding sources,” she said, according to Fortune.

    The wealth tax in the room

    There was a second reason, and it is personal. California voters decide on Proposition 40, a wealth tax, on November 3. As described by Investing.com, it is a one-time 5% levy on residents worth more than $1 billion, and Oppenheimer estimates Zuckerberg could face a $10 billion to $15 billion bill in 2027. Paying it by selling his ten-vote Class B shares would chip away at his control.

    Meta's filing says Zuckerberg told the board's special committee he was weighing a move out of the state. The deal that followed lets him sell up to 20% of the Class C shares he receives right away, representing 10% of his overall economic stake, “including so that he may pay his potential tax liability under California Proposition 40, if enacted into law, without affecting his majority voting control.” In return, Fortune reported, he dropped a request for a new pay package, kept his $1 salary and committed to stay in California, or wherever Meta is headquartered, for eight years.

    Shareholders get the new shares too, but not the votes. Meta's own filing warns that “the Class C common stock will likely trade at some level of discount to the Class A common stock,” which means Meta might have to issue more shares to raise the same money.

    Tesla went the other way, and that scared people too

    Meta was not alone on Wednesday. Tesla reported the same evening, and its net income fell 42.7% from a year earlier to $787 million, according to its 10-Q. On its call, Tesla said: “We're revising our CapEx guidance to be approximately $20 billion for the current year as a result of managing our capital deployment efficiently and plan to fund it primarily from our cash flows from operations,” according to a transcript published by The Motley Fool. Elon Musk said spending was “ending up being a little lower than we anticipated,” but that it “will start to become more meaningful next year,” reaching tens of billions of dollars in 2028.

    That is where Thursday's sell-off gets strange. Kyle Rodda, senior financial market analyst at Capital.com, said the fear had flipped. The risk investors saw, he said, is that companies are spending too little, “or more precisely, that future capex could be lower than previously thought and the rate of growth in investment could be slowing down,” TheStreet reported. In his reading, Meta “provided very little color about future plans, and Tesla reduced its full-year CAPEX guidance by 20%.”

    The chipmakers that sell to the builders took the worst of it. Intel and Advanced Micro Devices both fell about 8 percent and Broadcom slid 4.3 percent, Al Jazeera reported. Oil did not help: Brent crude rose 4% to $113.17 a barrel on Thursday after new Houthi strikes on Saudi oil infrastructure, according to Zacks. Not everyone saw a turning point. “In short, nothing has changed. Investors have just decided to be afraid this week,” Jay Goldberg, a senior analyst at Seaport Research Partners, told Al Jazeera.

    Readers were paying attention. Meta's Wikipedia page drew 50,512 page views this week, 89.8% more than its average over the previous four weeks.

    The week's insider-trading reports to the SEC add a footnote. Some of the largest insider sales came from technology companies: Snowflake directors Frank Slootman and Benoit Dageville sold $111.0 million of stock between them, UiPath's chief executive Daniel Dines sold $33.1 million, and Micron's Scott DeBoer sold $25.7 million. All four sales were made under pre-arranged 10b5-1 trading plans, and insiders sell for many reasons, from taxes to diversification. They are not a verdict on AI.

    Our read: the AI build-out is outgrowing its own cash

    This is analysis, not advice. Until recently, the biggest technology companies could pay for the AI boom out of the cash their ad, cloud and software businesses threw off. Meta's quarter is a sign that this phase is ending, at least for one of them. When spending outruns cash from operations, the money has to come from somewhere else: from savings, from lenders or from new shareholders. Meta has now built the machinery for all three, and the new share class is built so that outside money comes in without outside votes.

    That matters beyond Meta. If the companies building AI fund it more with debt and new shares, the risk of the build-out spreads from their own profits to bondholders, pension funds and anyone who owns an index fund. Thursday's sell-off showed the double bind: spending more worries investors about the cash, and spending less worries them about the boom. We think the second fear will keep winning as long as the chipmakers' sales depend on a handful of buyers.

    The next test arrives this week. Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon report, and Hargreaves Lansdown notes that third-quarter results are usually when the big spenders give a first steer on next year's capex. Our read would be wrong if they raise their spending plans while still producing healthy free cash flow, and if Meta's Class C shares, once they trade in January, sit close to the voting ones. Either would say the build-out can still pay its own way.

    For now, the clearest sign of how expensive AI has become may be this one: a company that earned billions of dollars a quarter just created a new kind of stock, and its founder promised to stay home for eight years.
