    What Lyft does

    Lyft runs a mobile app that connects riders with drivers for on-demand rides. Besides regular rideshare, the app offers taxis, private hire vehicles, executive chauffeur services, car sharing, and shared bikes and scooters. Drivers are independent people who choose when to drive, and riders use the app for commutes, errands and nights out.

    Substantially all of its revenue comes from its ridesharing marketplace, where it collects service fees and commissions from drivers. It also earns money from licensing and data access agreements, bike and scooter rentals, selling bikes and bike station equipment, advertising, renting cars to drivers through its Express Drive program, and selling its service to businesses. Lyft names Uber as its main ridesharing competitor, and Bolt as another rival in taxi and private hire apps.

    In 2025 Lyft expanded beyond North America by buying Freenow, a European taxi app, and TBR Global Chauffeuring, a luxury chauffeur company. It now operates in thousands of cities across six continents. At the end of 2025 it had 3,913 full-time employees worldwide. It also works with partners on self-driving vehicle technology.

    Its biggest risks, as it describes them

    • Competition from larger rivals, including Uber, and from companies building self-driving cars.
    • Failing to attract and keep drivers and riders at a reasonable cost.
    • Insurance losses larger than the amounts it has set aside, and reliance on a few insurance claims providers.
    • Relying on partners to bring self-driving vehicles to its platform, and the industry may not develop as hoped.
    • Its reliance on Amazon Web Services to deliver the app to users.
    From Lyft's annual report on Form 10-K, filed February 11, 2026, in our words; the 10-K has the full description and every risk factor. Updated with each new annual report.
