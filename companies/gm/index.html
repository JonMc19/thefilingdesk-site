    Value General Motors yourself

    A discounted cash flow (DCF) model values a business as the cash it may generate in future, discounted back to today. The starting figures come from General Motors' 10-K (or, for net cash and shares, its latest 10-Q) and the assumptions are yours: start from one of three example scenarios, then change any figure to see what it implies for a share, and how that compares with the last share price.

    Value per share on three example scenarios, from General Motors' fiscal 2025 free cash flow of $17.6B, net cash of −$103.9B at December 31, 2025 and 973 million diluted shares, from its filings. The scenarios are fitted to General Motors: the base scenario's growth in years 1 to 5 is its revenue growth over the last 3 years, 5.7% a year; the cautious and optimistic ones are lower and higher, and growth in years 6 to 10 is halfway to the long-run rate. The base scenario's discount rate is 11% for a cyclical or higher-risk business (Automobile Manufacturers); the cautious and optimistic ones use a point more and a point less.     How the model works. Free cash flow grows at the first rate for five years and the second rate for five more, and each year is discounted at the discount rate. Everything after year 10 is valued as year-10 cash flow × (1 + growth after year 10) ÷ (discount rate − growth after year 10), discounted back ten years. Net cash is added and the total is divided by diluted shares. The result depends entirely on the assumptions. It is not a forecast, a price target or a recommendation.     The price-to-earnings (P/E) method

    Another common way to value a company is to multiply its earnings per share by a price-to-earnings (P/E) multiple: the number of years of today's earnings an investor pays for a share.     How the P/E method works. Value per share is earnings per share times the multiple. On a future year's earnings, that gives the price in that year, which is brought back to today at the discount rate: price ÷ (1 + discount rate) for each year ahead. It is quick and easy to compare across companies, but it ignores debt and cash, and earnings can swing with one-off gains and charges, so a multiple says most about steady, profitable businesses. Like the model above, the result depends entirely on the multiple you choose. It is not a forecast, a price target or a recommendation.
