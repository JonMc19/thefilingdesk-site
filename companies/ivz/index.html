    Invesco

    IVZ · NYSE · Financials · Financials, fiscal 2016 to 2025

    Invesco's revenue rose 5.1% to $6.4 billion in fiscal 2025. Its operating margin was −10.9%, down from 13.7% a year earlier. Free cash flow was $1.4 billion, up 28.6%. It paid out $501 million in dividends and buybacks, 35% of free cash flow. The diluted share count fell 0.6%.

    Value Invesco yourself

    A discounted cash flow (DCF) model values a business as the cash it may generate in future, discounted back to today. The starting figures come from Invesco's 10-K (or, for net cash and shares, its latest 10-Q) and the assumptions are yours: start from one of three example scenarios, then change any figure to see what it implies for a share.

    Value per share on three example scenarios, from Invesco's fiscal 2025 free cash flow of $1.4B, net cash of −$788M at December 31, 2025 and 455 million diluted shares, from its filings. The scenarios are fitted to Invesco: the base scenario's growth in years 1 to 5 is its revenue growth over the last 3 years, 1.8% a year; the cautious and optimistic ones are lower and higher, and growth in years 6 to 10 is halfway to the long-run rate. The base scenario's discount rate is 10%: 9% for a business of moderate risk (Asset Management & Custody Banks), plus 1 point for unsteady results (a loss in fiscal 2025); the cautious and optimistic ones use a point more and a point less.     How the model works. Free cash flow grows at the first rate for five years and the second rate for five more, and each year is discounted at the discount rate. Everything after year 10 is valued as year-10 cash flow × (1 + growth after year 10) ÷ (discount rate − growth after year 10), discounted back ten years. Net cash is added and the total is divided by diluted shares. The result depends entirely on the assumptions. It is not a forecast, a price target or a recommendation.

    The price-to-earnings (P/E) method

    Another common way to value a company is to multiply its earnings per share by a price-to-earnings (P/E) multiple: the number of years of today's earnings an investor pays for a share. A higher multiple usually means investors expect earnings to grow. Choose a multiple, for example from similar companies, to see the value it gives.

    The P/E method needs positive earnings, and Invesco's latest diluted earnings per share were negative or zero.
