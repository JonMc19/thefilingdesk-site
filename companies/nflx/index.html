    Value Netflix yourself

    Netflix's revenue rose 15.8% to $45.2 billion in fiscal 2025. Its operating margin was 29.5%, up from 26.7% a year earlier. Free cash flow was $9.5 billion, up 36.7%. It paid out $9.1 billion in dividends and buybacks, 96% of free cash flow. The diluted share count fell 1.1%.

    What Netflix does

    Netflix runs a paid streaming service with TV series, films, games and live programming in many genres and languages. Members can watch as much as they want, whenever they want, and change their plan at any time. It offers a range of pricing plans, including one with advertising.

    Most of its revenue comes from monthly membership fees, and it reports a single business segment. Netflix says it competes for people's free time not only with other streaming services and traditional TV, but also with video games, sites where people post their own videos, and social media. It also competes with them for the rights to films and shows, both licensed and its own productions.

    At the end of 2025 Netflix had about 16,000 full-time employees, 68% of them in the United States and Canada. It also employs people in content production, some part-time or temporary.

    Its biggest risks, as it describes them

    • Failing to attract and keep members, or to keep improving the service for them.
    • Competition from other video services, and the cost and legal risks of the shows and films it buys or makes.
    • Large, long-term content commitments and debt that leave it less room to adjust spending.
    • Its reliance on Amazon Web Services and on the devices of its partners to deliver the service.
    • The WBD transaction may not be completed as planned, or may not bring the benefits Netflix expects.

    1Discounted cash flow (DCF)

    A discounted cash flow (DCF) model values a business as the cash it may generate in future, discounted back to today. The starting figures come from Netflix's 10-K (or, for net cash and shares, its latest 10-Q) and the assumptions are yours: start from one of three example scenarios, then change any figure to see what it implies for a share, and how that compares with the last share price.

    Value per share on three example scenarios, from Netflix's cash from operations minus depreciation in fiscal 2025 (free cash flow before spending to grow: capital spending was 2.1 times depreciation, which holds its free cash flow down) of $9.8B, net cash of −$5.4B at December 31, 2025 and 4,344 million diluted shares, from its filings. The scenarios are fitted to Netflix: the base scenario's growth in years 1 to 5 is its revenue growth over the last 3 years, 12.6% a year; the cautious and optimistic ones are lower and higher, and growth in years 6 to 10 is halfway to the long-run rate. The base scenario's discount rate is 9% for a business of moderate risk (Movies & Entertainment); the cautious and optimistic ones use a point more and a point less.     How the model works. Free cash flow grows at the first rate for five years and the second rate for five more, and each year is discounted at the discount rate.     2Price-to-earnings (P/E) method

    Another common way to value a company is to multiply its earnings per share by a price-to-earnings (P/E) multiple: the number of years of today's earnings an investor pays for a share. A higher multiple usually means investors expect earnings to grow. Choose a multiple, for example from similar companies, to see the value it gives.

    Value per share on three example scenarios, from Netflix's earnings per share of $3.18 (over the last four quarters). Each grows earnings for a year at the cash flow scenarios' rates and applies a multiple to next year's earnings: the peers' median P/E of 22.3× for the base scenario, 20% less and more for the cautious and optimistic ones. The price is brought back a year at 10%, 9% and 8%. Select one to load it into the calculator. These are the model's arithmetic on those assumptions, not a forecast or a price target.

    How the P/E method works. Value per share is earnings per share times the multiple. On a future year's earnings, that gives the price in that year, which is brought back to today at the discount rate: price ÷ (1 + discount rate) for each year ahead. It is quick and easy to compare across companies, but it ignores debt and cash, and earnings can swing with one-off gains and charges, so a multiple says most about steady, profitable businesses. Like the cash flow model, the result depends entirely on the assumptions you choose. It is not a forecast, a price target or a recommendation.
