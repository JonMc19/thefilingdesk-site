    What Delta Air Lines does

    Delta Air Lines flies passengers and cargo across the United States and to six continents. Its main hubs are Atlanta, Detroit, Minneapolis-St. Paul and Salt Lake City, with coastal hubs in Boston, Los Angeles, New York and Seattle. Delta says it carried over 200 million customers in 2025, using a fleet of 1,314 aircraft at year end. Regional airlines, including its own Endeavor Air, fly smaller planes under the Delta Connection name.

    Most of its money comes from ticket sales, and Delta is putting more seats in its pricier cabins, such as Delta One and First Class. Its SkyMiles loyalty program is a large second source: Delta sells miles to partners, above all American Express, whose co-branded credit card brought in $8.2 billion in 2025. It also earns money from cargo, from repairing engines and aircraft for other airlines through Delta TechOps, and from Delta Vacations. Delta says the airline industry is highly competitive.

    Abroad, Delta works through joint ventures with Air France-KLM, Virgin Atlantic, Aeromexico, LATAM and Korean Air, and owns stakes in several of these carriers. It also owns Monroe Energy, which runs an oil refinery near Philadelphia that supplies jet fuel to its northeastern operations. Delta has more than 100,000 employees.

    Its biggest risks, as it describes them

    • A crash or other serious accident involving its planes or those of its partner airlines, bringing losses and bad publicity.
    • High or rising jet fuel prices, or a long break in fuel supply, including from its own refinery.
    • Its partnerships with and stakes in foreign airlines may not deliver the results Delta expects.
    • Strikes or other labor disruptions among its workforce.
    • An accident or pollution problem at the Monroe refinery that insurance does not fully cover.
    From Delta Air Lines's annual report on Form 10-K, filed February 11, 2026, in our words; the 10-K has the full description and every risk factor. Updated with each new annual report.
