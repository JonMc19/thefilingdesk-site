    What Blaize does

    Blaize designs computing hardware and software for AI inference, the stage where a trained AI model is put to work. Its systems are meant to run at the edge, close to where data is collected, and in data centers. Local processing can cut bandwidth use and help in applications that need real-time decisions.

    Its own hardware is the GSP, a programmable AI processor, and compute cards that carry it. It also sells third-party hardware, mainly servers and small stand-alone systems that run its technology. On the software side it offers a development kit and Blaize AI Studio, a no-code or low-code tool for building and deploying AI models. It also sells consulting services to configure its hardware and licensed technology for a customer.

    Blaize says a substantial part of its 2025 revenue came from third-party hardware, which carries lower margins than its own products. Its revenue comes from a limited number of large customers, some of them related parties. It relies on outside manufacturers, including Samsung Foundry and Plexus, to build its chips. It became public in January 2025 by merging with BurTech Acquisition Corporation, a blank-check company, and is based in El Dorado Hills, California.

    Its biggest risks, as it describes them

    • It has a history of operating losses and may not reach profitability.
    • It depends on a small number of customers, some of them related parties, for much of its revenue.
    • Much of its revenue comes from third-party hardware that earns lower margins than its own products.
    • Its next-generation chip may be delayed or not built because of limited funds.
    • It depends on a few suppliers and outside manufacturers, including Samsung Foundry and Plexus.
