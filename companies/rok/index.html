    What Rockwell Automation does

    Rockwell Automation sells the equipment, software and services that factories and other industrial sites use to automate production. Its Allen-Bradley brand traces back to 1903, and the current company was formed in 1996 when it split from the former Rockwell International. Customers range across many industries and sell goods that are made in discrete steps or in continuous processes.

    The company has three operating segments. Intelligent Devices covers drives, motion control, material handling, safety and sensing products and other industrial components. Software & Control covers control and visualization software and hardware, simulation and digital twin software, and network and security infrastructure. Lifecycle Services covers consulting, engineered solutions, and recurring services such as cybersecurity, remote monitoring and asset management, plus the Sensia joint venture.

    The segments share sales, supply chain and support teams and work globally. Rockwell relies on a distribution channel for a substantial part of its sales. It says its competitors range from large, diversified corporations to smaller firms with limited industrial automation portfolios, and that it is not subject to significant seasonality.

    Its biggest risks, as it describes them

    • Falling sales and profit if economic or industry conditions weaken.
    • Tariffs and other trade policy changes that could hurt its profit and competitiveness.
    • Its reliance on a distribution channel for a substantial part of its sales.
    • Failing to respond to changes in what customers want from automation products.
    • Inherent risks in its solutions and services businesses.
    From Rockwell Automation's annual report on Form 10-K, filed November 12, 2025, in our words; the 10-K has the full description and every risk factor. Updated with each new annual report.
