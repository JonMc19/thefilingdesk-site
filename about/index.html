    About The Filing Desk

    The Filing Desk publishes short reports on what listed companies' results show. Each one starts from the company's own filings with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), sets out what changed in the business, and links to every source.

    Where the numbers come from

    • Annual and quarterly reports (Forms 10-K and 10-Q) filed with the SEC, taken from the structured XBRL data on EDGAR.
    • Company earnings releases, for items the filings don't break out.

    When a later filing restates an earlier year, the reports use the latest figure the company filed.

    How figures are calculated

    • Free cash flow is net cash from operating activities minus additions to property and equipment.
    • Margins are the item divided by revenue for the same period.
    • Years are the company's fiscal years, which may not match calendar years.

    Each report lists any other definitions it uses.

    Valuation tools

    Every company page, except for banks, has a discounted cash flow model for valuing the company yourself. It starts from the company's own figures in its latest 10-K: free cash flow (the latest year, a three-year average, or cash from operations minus depreciation as a guide to cash flow before spending to grow), net cash and diluted shares, with the option of taking net cash and shares from its latest quarterly report instead. A company building heavily, whose capital spending is more than one and a half times its depreciation, starts from cash flow before growth spending, and a share count that changed sharply since the 10-K (after a stock split, say) is taken from the latest quarter. You choose the assumptions, or start from one of three example scenarios fitted to the company: the base scenario's growth for the first five years is the company's own revenue growth over the last three years (kept between 0% and 20%), the cautious and optimistic ones are lower and higher. The base scenario's discount rate follows fixed rules: 7% for a stable business (utilities, consumer staples, telecom carriers, insurers, drugmakers and health services), 11% for a cyclical or higher-risk one (energy, materials, chipmakers, biotech, carmakers, airlines, homebuilders, casinos and hotels, investment banks), 9% for the rest, plus a point for a company worth under $10 billion and a point for unsteady results (a loss in any of the last five years, or revenue falling in two of them), kept between 6% and 13%; the cautious and optimistic scenarios use a point more and a point less. The page shows what they imply for a share, how that compares with the last closing share price, and the growth that price implies.

    Closing share prices are consolidated US end-of-day prices from Databento, updated after each trading day. The model's results depend entirely on the assumptions you choose. They are not forecasts, price targets or recommendations, and The Filing Desk doesn't publish its own.

    Below the model, the price-to-earnings (P/E) method values a share as its earnings per share times a multiple you choose, with the company's own P/E at the last close, its peers' median and its sector's median as guides. You can apply the multiple to today's earnings or to your own estimate for any of the next five years, whose price is then brought back to today at a discount rate. Earnings per share come from the 10-K or the last four quarters, and are put on today's share count after a stock split. Banks, whose cash flow is mostly lending, get the P/E method only.

    What the reports don't do

    They don't cover share prices, valuation or analyst forecasts, and they don't rate stocks or recommend buying, selling or holding anything. They describe what companies reported.

    The week in filings

    The Sunday story is different from the reports. It takes the week's biggest business and markets topic and explains it using news coverage, public posts, market data and the filings, with analysis of what it could mean next. Every fact is attributed and every figure is checked against its source before publishing, and passages that look ahead are marked as analysis. Like everything here, they never recommend buying or selling anything.

    Photos

    Photos come from Wikimedia Commons, under licences that allow anyone to reuse them. Each one is credited under the photo with its author and licence. They show the companies, places and people in a story; they aren't taken at the events described unless the caption says so.

    Corrections

    If a figure doesn't match the filing, open an issue on the site's GitHub page. Confirmed errors are corrected in the report, with a note saying what changed.
